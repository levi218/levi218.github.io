3:T238f,<p><em>About a 6-minute read</em></p>
<p>A model can only write text. A <strong>tool</strong> is how that text turns into action: the model writes a request — &quot;look up client 1042&#39;s invoices&quot; — and the software around it carries the request out and hands back the result.</p>
<h2>A brain with no hands</h2>
<p>On its own, a model cannot open your spreadsheet, check today&#39;s exchange rate or send an email. It can only describe what it would do. Tools close that gap.</p>
<p>Think of a capable manager who works by phone from a room with no computer. They cannot touch the files, but they can say &quot;pull up the March invoices&quot; or &quot;email the client&quot;, and an assistant outside the room does it and reads back what happened. The model is the manager. The software around it — the app, or later the agent — is the assistant with the keyboard.</p>
<h2>What a tool actually is</h2>
<p>A tool is a single, clearly labelled action, described in plain words so the model knows when to use it. Each one has three parts:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A name</strong> — for example, &quot;look up invoices&quot;.</li>
<li><strong>A description</strong> — what it does, when to use it and what it returns.</li>
<li><strong>Inputs</strong> — what it needs to run, such as a client number and a month.</li>
</ul>
<p>The model is handed the whole list at the start, like a menu. It reads the descriptions and decides which item, if any, the current step needs. That is why a clear tool description matters as much as a clear instruction: a vague label gets picked at the wrong moment, or never.</p>
<p>Common tools include web search, reading and writing files, a calculator, a calendar, email, a database or accounting system lookup, and running a small piece of code.</p>

The model does not run anything itself: it writes a structured request, and the app decides whether to carry it out. The result then goes back on the model&#39;s desk (Post 2), and the model either answers or asks for another tool. A single question can involve several rounds of this.</p>
<p>This split is what makes tools safe enough for business. The app can refuse a request, ask a person to approve it first, or limit which records the model may see.</p>

<p>A useful rule: the harder an action is to undo, the closer a person should stand to it. Many successful agents can read almost anything but can only <em>draft</em> the actions that leave the building.</p>
<h2>Tools also reduce hallucination</h2>
<p>Remember the weaknesses in Post 1: arithmetic, recent facts, private information. Tools fix all three. Instead of guessing a total, the model calls a calculator. Instead of recalling an exchange rate from training, it looks today&#39;s rate up. Instead of inventing a client&#39;s address, it reads it from your records.</p>
<p>When you design an agent, a good habit is to ask of every fact it needs: should the model <em>know</em> this, or should it <em>look it up</em>? Anything that changes, or anything that must be exact, should be looked up.</p>
<h2>Linh lists her tools</h2>
<p>Linh walks through her receipt process and writes down every action a person takes. Her list:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Read</strong> new receipts from the client&#39;s email or shared folder.</li>
<li><strong>Look up</strong> the client&#39;s own category list and tax settings.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate</strong> totals and convert foreign-currency receipts at the official rate.</li>
<li><strong>Write</strong> one row per receipt into the client&#39;s expense spreadsheet.</li>
<li><strong>Draft</strong> an email to the client listing receipts that are missing or unreadable.</li>
</ul>
<p>She marks the last one &quot;draft only&quot;: a person on her team will press Send. Everything else is either reading or easy to undo.</p>
<h2>Try it on your task</h2>
<p>For the task you have been following, list every action a person takes in a system: opening, searching, calculating, writing, sending. Label each one Read, Calculate, Change or Send. The Send items are where you will place a human check in Post 7.</p>
