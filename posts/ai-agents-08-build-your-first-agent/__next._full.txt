fill="var(--cds-text-secondary)">improve instructions and skills, then retest</text></svg></figure><p>The first six steps are design decisions you can make on paper. Step 7, the test set, is highlighted because it is the one most often skipped — and without it you cannot tell whether a change made things better or worse.</p>
<h2>Define</h2>
<p><strong>1. Pick one narrow task.</strong> Not &quot;automate my accounting&quot; but &quot;turn a client&#39;s monthly receipts into a categorised expense sheet&quot;. A good first task is <em>frequent</em> (it happens every week), <em>bounded</em> (it has a clear start and end), <em>checkable</em> (a person can tell quickly whether the result is right) and <em>valuable</em> (someone would pay to stop doing it). Avoid tasks where one mistake would be very costly; you can move to those later.</p>
<p><strong>2. Define what &quot;good&quot; looks like.</strong> Write down the finished output in exact terms: which fields, which format, what counts as an error. Then set a bar, such as &quot;every amount matches the receipt; categories match what our senior accountant would choose at least as often as a junior would&quot;. If you cannot describe good, the agent cannot hit it, and you cannot measure it.</p>
<p><strong>3. Map how the expert does it today.</strong> Sit with the person who does the task best and write down every step, every decision and every exception they handle — including the ones they do without thinking. This map becomes your workflow, your skills and your instructions. It is also where most of the value lies: the AI is available to everyone, but this map is yours.</p>
<h2>Design</h2>
<p><strong>4. Shape the work.</strong> Using Post 7, decide which steps follow a fixed route and which need an agent to decide. Choose the patterns. Place the checkpoints: a gate before anything that leaves the building, an exception queue for anything uncertain. Choose a starting autonomy level from Post 6 — usually level 2.</p>
<p><strong>5. Gather the parts.</strong> From the map, list:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tools</strong> the agent needs, marked read or act (Post 3), and whether a connector exists for each (Post 4).</li>
<li><strong>Skills</strong> for the procedures that need expertise (Post 5).</li>
<li><strong>Knowledge</strong> the agent must consult: rules, client profiles, templates.</li>
</ul>
<p>Give each tool the narrowest access that does the job.</p>
<p><strong>6. Write the instructions.</strong> Turn your Post 2 briefing into the agent&#39;s job description: purpose, the steps at a high level, the output format, when to use which skill, and above all what to do when unsure. Keep it short; detailed procedures belong in skills.</p>
<p>How you put these parts together depends on your situation. Some people use an AI assistant that already supports connectors and skills; some use a no-code agent builder; some work with a developer. The design decisions above are the same whichever route you take, and they are the part that determines quality.</p>
<h2>Prove</h2>
<p><strong>7. Build a test set.</strong> Collect 30 to 100 real past cases with the correct answer for each — here, receipts your team has already processed correctly. Include the awkward ones: faded, handwritten, foreign currency, duplicates. Run the agent on all of them and score the results against your definition of good. Every time you change the instructions or a skill, run the test set again. This turns &quot;it seems better&quot; into &quot;it got 94 out of 100 right, up from 88&quot;.</p>
<p><strong>8. Run a pilot.</strong> Use the agent on live work, but with a person checking every output before it goes anywhere. A gentle way to start is to run it <strong>alongside</strong> your team for a few weeks: both process the same work, and you compare. Every disagreement teaches you something, and the best of them become new test cases.</p>
<p><strong>9. Measure, improve and widen.</strong> Track a handful of numbers each week:</p>

<h2>Linh&#39;s pilot</h2>
<p>Linh runs her agent alongside her team for one month on five friendly clients. Her test set catches an early problem: restaurant receipts that include service charges were being split wrongly. Thu adds two lines to the &quot;Read a receipt&quot; skill, the test set confirms the fix, and the problem does not return.</p>
<p>By the end of the month her team is reviewing exceptions rather than typing, and she has the numbers to decide whether this can become a product. That decision is Post 9.</p>
