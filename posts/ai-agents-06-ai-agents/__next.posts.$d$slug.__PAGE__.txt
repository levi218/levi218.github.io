3:T29f8,<p><em>About an 8-minute read</em></p>
<p>An <strong>AI agent</strong> is a model given a goal, tools and know-how, and left to work in a loop: decide the next step, take it, check the result, and repeat until the goal is met or it needs a person. The loop is what separates an agent from a chat assistant.</p>
<h2>From answering to doing</h2>
<p>Ask a chat assistant &quot;what should I do about these 40 receipts?&quot; and you get advice. Give an agent the goal &quot;process this month&#39;s receipts for client 1042&quot; and it opens the folder, reads each receipt, looks up the client&#39;s categories, writes the rows, notices three are missing and drafts an email about them. It does not stop after one reply; it keeps going until the job is done.</p>
<p>That difference — a single answer versus a finished piece of work — is why agents matter for anyone who wants to sell outcomes rather than advice.</p>

instructions · tools · skills · knowledge · notes from earlier steps</text></g></svg></figure><p>Each pass round the loop is one small step. The model looks at its desk — the goal, its instructions, what it has done so far — and decides what to do next. It asks for a tool. The result lands back on its desk. It checks whether the goal is now met. If not, round it goes again.</p>
<p>Two exits matter as much as the loop itself. <strong>Deliver</strong> when the work is complete. <strong>Ask a person</strong> when it is unsure, when something does not match, or when the next step is one you have marked as needing approval.</p>
<h2>The recipe</h2>
<p>You have already met every ingredient:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A model</strong> — the brain (Post 1).</li>
<li><strong>Instructions</strong> — the job description and the rules (Post 2).</li>
<li><strong>Tools</strong> — the actions it may take, often through connectors (Posts 3 and 4).</li>
<li><strong>Skills and knowledge</strong> — your procedures and your documents (Post 5).</li>
<li><strong>The loop</strong> — software that keeps passing results back to the model and decides when to stop. People sometimes call this the <strong>harness</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Stopping rules</strong> — when to deliver, when to ask, and when to give up.</li>
</ul>
<p>The model is the part everyone talks about, but the other five are the parts you design. They are also what makes one agent reliable and another a liability.</p>
<h2>Linh&#39;s agent, step by step</h2>
<p>Here is one real pass through a client&#39;s month, with each loop marked:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Decide:</strong> &quot;I should see what arrived.&quot; <strong>Act:</strong> list new files in the client&#39;s folder. <strong>Check:</strong> 38 receipts.</li>
<li><strong>Decide:</strong> &quot;Read the first receipt using the &#39;Read a receipt&#39; skill.&quot; <strong>Act:</strong> read it. <strong>Check:</strong> a hotel bill that includes a dinner.</li>
<li><strong>Decide:</strong> &quot;The skill says split hotel and meals.&quot; <strong>Act:</strong> write two rows. <strong>Check:</strong> rows saved.</li>
<li>The same pattern repeats for the other receipts. Two are faded: it marks them UNCLEAR instead of guessing.</li>
<li><strong>Decide:</strong> &quot;Compare with the client&#39;s bank statement.&quot; <strong>Act:</strong> read the statement. <strong>Check:</strong> three payments have no receipt.</li>
<li><strong>Decide:</strong> &quot;Draft the missing-receipts email.&quot; <strong>Act:</strong> create a draft, not send it. <strong>Check:</strong> done?</li>
<li><strong>Exit: ask a person.</strong> It hands Linh&#39;s team a summary: 36 receipts filed, 2 unclear, 3 missing, email waiting for approval.</li>
</ol>
<p>Notice that nobody told the agent the order of steps 5 and 6. It worked them out from the goal and the skills. That flexibility is the strength of an agent, and also its risk.</p>
<h2>How much freedom to give</h2>
<p>Autonomy is a dial, not a switch. Most businesses should move along it slowly, on evidence.</p>

<thead>
<tr>
<th>Level</th>
<th>The agent...</th>

<th>Good for</th>
</tr>

<tbody><tr>

<td>drafts or recommends</td>
<td>does the work, using the draft</td>

<tr>

<td>prepares each action</td>
<td>approves or edits before it happens</td>
<td>Most new services</td>

<p>Start at level 2. Move a task up a level only when your records show the agent gets it right reliably, and keep anything irreversible — sending, paying, deleting — at level 2 for longest.</p>
<h2>Guardrails</h2>
<p>Guardrails are the limits you build around the loop so that a mistake stays small:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Narrow tools.</strong> Give only the actions the job needs, read-only where possible.</li>
<li><strong>Approval points.</strong> Name the steps that always need a person, and make the agent stop there.</li>
<li><strong>Budgets.</strong> A maximum number of steps, minutes or cost per job, so a confused agent cannot loop forever.</li>
<li><strong>A log.</strong> A written record of every step, tool call and result. It is how you find out what went wrong, and how you prove to a client what was done.</li>
<li><strong>&quot;Ask when unsure.&quot;</strong> Written into the instructions, and rewarded rather than treated as failure.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Where agents still slip</h2>
<p>Agents are improving fast, but a few weak spots are worth designing around:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Long tasks drift.</strong> The longer the loop runs, the more chances for a small early mistake to grow.</li>
<li><strong>They can get stuck,</strong> retrying the same failing step.</li>
<li><strong>They can be confidently wrong,</strong> just like the model inside them.</li>
<li><strong>Every pass costs time and money.</strong> An agent that wanders uses more of both.</li>
</ul>
<p>The cure for most of these is not a smarter agent but a better-designed piece of work: breaking the job into clear stages, with checks between them. That is the subject of Post 7.</p>
<h2>Try it on your task</h2>
<p>Write the goal for your agent in one sentence, starting with a verb: &quot;Process...&quot;, &quot;Prepare...&quot;, &quot;Answer...&quot;. Then choose its starting autonomy level from the table, and list the steps that must always stop for a person.</p>
