An AI-powered service sells a finished outcome — a checked expense sheet, a translated contract, a qualified list of leads — that an agent produces and a responsible person stands behind. You are not selling the AI. You are selling the result: faster, more consistent, and at a price the old way could not reach.

## The menu: what you can actually sell

A finished outcome is one way to make money from agents, but not the only one. Seven kinds of offer are selling in 2026. Most practices start with one and add a second once the first is running.

| Offer | What the client gets | Typical buyer | How it is priced |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Packaged agent for a small business** | One narrow agent set up and looked after: answers FAQs, books appointments, qualifies leads, chases invoices | Shops, clinics, agencies, trades — owners with no IT team | Small setup fee plus a monthly care plan |
| **2. Custom agent or workflow build** | An agent designed around one of the client's own processes, connected to their systems | Growing firms with a specific, costly process | Project fee, then a monthly fee for running costs and upkeep |
| **3. AI-run service** | The finished work itself — this post's main topic | Anyone who would otherwise hire staff or outsource | Subscription, per item or per outcome |
| **4. MCP server or connector** | A door that lets AI assistants safely read and act on a system — the client's own, or yours | Companies wanting their AI to work with real data; software and data owners | Build fee for a custom server; subscription and usage tiers for a packaged one |
| **5. Skills and knowledge packs** | Your know-how written as skills, checklists and reference libraries (Post 5) that others plug into their own agents | Peers in your profession; teams already using AI | Licence or subscription, updated when the rules change |
| **6. Implementation and embedded engineering** | A person who sits with the client's team and takes a pilot all the way to production | Mid-size and large organisations stuck at "pilot" | Day rate or a fixed fee per deployed workflow |
| **7. Training, evaluation and assurance** | Workshops, test sets (Post 8), audits of how an organisation's AI is checked and governed | Managers, regulated industries, procurement teams | Per course, per seat or per audit |

Some reference points from published 2026 price guides — broad ranges, not quotes, and they vary a lot by country and scope:

- **Starter packaged agents** — one bot, one channel, a basic knowledge base, a month of support — are commonly offered at about $500–$2,000 set-up, with local-business care plans from roughly $300–$500 a month.
- **A narrow custom agent built around one real workflow** is quoted at roughly $8,000–$45,000 to build, plus $150–$1,500 a month for model usage, hosting and upkeep.
- **Embedded engineering** is one of the fastest-growing roles in the industry: job postings for it on one large job board rose from about 640 to about 5,300 between 2025 and 2026, and large cloud providers and consultancies are building whole departments of these engineers.

The pattern behind the table: offers 1, 2 and 6 sell your *time*, so they are easy to start and hard to scale. Offers 3, 4 and 5 sell something you build once and deliver many times, so they are harder to start and much better to own. A sensible path is to start with a time-based offer, notice which job you keep rebuilding, and turn that job into a packaged one.

## Head to head: a small-business agent or an enterprise MCP server?

These two come up most often, and they are almost opposite businesses.

|  | Agent for a small business | Enterprise MCP server |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What you build** | A narrow agent for one job, often on an existing platform | A secure door between AI assistants and the company's internal systems |
| **Who decides** | The owner, often in one meeting | IT, security, data owners and a budget holder |
| **Sales cycle** | Days to weeks | Months |
| **What wins the deal** | A visible result: missed calls answered, bookings made, invoices chased | Security, permissions, audit trail, reliability under load |
| **Deal size** | Hundreds to a few thousand dollars, then a monthly fee | Tens of thousands for a build, then support or a platform fee |
| **Biggest risk** | Many small clients, each wanting changes; churn | Long sales, heavy security review, and the server becoming infrastructure you must run for years |
| **Moat** | Niche know-how and a reputation in one trade | Deep knowledge of one system or industry, plus a track record in production |

**What enterprises actually pay for in MCP.** Connecting a model to an API is the easy part; plenty of public servers already exist for popular tools. Companies pay for the hard parts around it: servers tailored to their internal systems under strict security and governance rules, sign-in and permissions that work across many servers, and a record of what each AI assistant did. Industry write-ups describe sign-in as the main source of friction today, and warn of servers wired into production with no audit trail. A buyer guide published this year also notes that many firms claiming MCP expertise have only built thin wrappers — so one server running in a real business is worth more in a sale than any slide.

**The product version.** Instead of a new custom build for every client, some founders package a reliable connector for one popular platform or one industry, then charge a subscription with usage tiers. A common path is services first, product second: the custom projects show you which connector everyone needs.

**Which one fits you?** If you know one trade well and can sell face to face, start with small-business agents. If you understand one complex system — an ERP, a laboratory information system, a government data platform — and can speak to security teams, an MCP server for that system is the rarer and more defensible offer. Linh's plan from Post 4, a connector that lets clients' assistants ask questions of their books, is the small-scale version of the second path.

## AI-assisted or AI-first

There are two broad ways to put agents into a service.

- **AI-assisted.** People still do the work; AI drafts, researches and checks, so each person handles more. The service looks the same to the client. Your margins improve, but the price usually stays close to the traditional one.
- **AI-first.** The workflow does the work end to end, and people handle exceptions, approvals and accountability. This allows new price points, faster turnaround and clients who could never afford the service before.

Which one fits depends on two things: how repetitive the work is, and how much a mistake costs.

## What exactly you are selling

Clients do not buy "an agent". They buy a promise. Write it down as five parts:

1. **The outcome.** "Your month's receipts, turned into a categorised expense sheet ready for your accountant or tax filing."
2. **Who it is for.** "Small businesses with up to a few hundred receipts a month."
3. **Turnaround.** How quickly the result arrives after the client sends their material.
4. **The quality promise.** What you check, who checks it, and what happens if something is wrong.
5. **What the client must do.** Send receipts to one address; answer questions about unclear items.

Notice that none of these mention AI. The AI is how you keep the promise, not the promise itself.

## Pricing

Price on the value to the client, not on what the AI costs you. A client compares your fee with the hours their own staff spend, or with what a traditional provider charges, not with your bill for AI usage.

| Pricing model | How it works | Fits when | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Subscription by volume tier | A monthly fee covering up to a set number of items | Steady, recurring work | Set a fair overage price for busy months |
| Per item or per job | A price per receipt, document or report | Irregular volume | Clients may hold work back to save money |
| Per project | A fixed price for a defined deliverable | One-off jobs with clear scope | Scope creep; define what is included |
| Share of the outcome | A percentage of money saved or recovered | The result is easy to measure | Disputes over how the result is counted |

Whichever you choose, know your **cost per job**: AI usage, plus the minutes your people spend reviewing, plus your share of overheads. Post 8's measures give you these figures. Review time is usually the biggest cost, which is why reducing exceptions improves your margin more than switching to a cheaper model.

Always include the human review in the price, and say so. It is a feature, not an overhead.

## Earning a client's trust

For many clients, handing work to "the AI" is a leap. Make it a small step:

- **Be open about how it works.** Say that AI does the first pass and where a qualified person checks. Most clients are reassured by a clear explanation, and unsettled by finding out later.
- **Handle their data with care.** Decide what you keep, for how long, where it is stored and who can see it. Use AI services and connectors whose data terms you have read, and never let client data train someone else's model without consent. Check your local data-protection rules and any rules for your profession; this series is not legal advice.
- **Stand behind the result.** Name who is accountable, and promise a fast correction if something is wrong.
- **Show your evidence.** Share your accuracy figures from the pilot. Few competitors measure, so numbers set you apart.
- **Keep the trail.** The logs from Post 6 let you show a client exactly what was done with their material, and when.

## What protects your business

The AI model is not your advantage: every competitor can use the same one, and it gets cheaper and better every year. What they cannot copy quickly is:

- **Your know-how,** written into skills and refined by real cases.
- **Your test set,** which lets you improve safely and prove quality.
- **Your workflow design,** with its checkpoints in the right places.
- **Your relationships and reputation** in a niche you understand.
- **Your accountability** — credentials, a signature, a person the client can call.

There is a pleasant side-effect. Because your value lives in these, every improvement in AI models makes your service faster and cheaper to run without making it easier to copy. Re-run your test set whenever you change model, and adopt the new one when it scores at least as well.

## Keeping it running

Once clients depend on you, a little routine goes a long way:

- **Weekly:** look at the exceptions and corrections. Repeated ones point to a skill that needs a new rule.
- **When rules change:** update the knowledge library and the affected skills, and add test cases for the change.
- **Monthly:** review your Post 8 measures per client, and decide whether any stage has earned more autonomy.

## Linh launches

Linh calls her service "Receipts in, books out". Clients forward receipts to a dedicated address or drop them in a shared folder. Within the promised turnaround they receive a categorised sheet and a short report: what was filed, what is unclear, what is missing. A qualified member of her team reviews every exception and approves every client email. Pricing is a monthly subscription in three volume tiers.

Her first ten clients come from her existing network, and her pilot accuracy figures do most of the selling. Six months in, she revisits the idea from Post 4: a connector that lets clients' own AI assistants ask questions of their books. The same know-how, sold a second way.

## Try it on your task

Write your offer in one paragraph: who it is for, the outcome, the turnaround, what a person checks, and how you will price it. Then place it on the 2×2 above. If it lands in "AI-first", design for spot-checks; if it lands in "AI-first with gates", make the expert's sign-off the heart of what you sell.

That paragraph, your Post 8 canvas and your test set are everything you need to start a pilot with your first client.
